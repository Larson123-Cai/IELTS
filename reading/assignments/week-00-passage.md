## Do more gyms make a city healthier?

**A**
In many countries, public health has become a political issue as well as a medical one. Rates of obesity, diabetes and heart disease have risen, and governments are under pressure to “do something visible”. One popular response is to spend more on sports facilities: swimming pools, indoor gyms and outdoor pitches. The idea seems straightforward. If people have somewhere convenient to exercise, they will exercise more, and the population will become healthier. Several cities have therefore opened new centres in the last decade and presented them as a public-health success.

**B**
The evidence is less comfortable. Evaluations in more than one city have found that new centres are used mainly by people who already take exercise — office workers who want a better gym, or amateur clubs that needed a place to train. People who do little or no physical activity, the group that public-health policy most needs to reach, often do not walk through the door. Distance is one reason, but not the only one. Cost, opening hours, childcare and a feeling of not “belonging” in a sports space all matter. In other words, building a facility removes one barrier; it does not remove the rest.

**C**
None of this means that sports centres are useless. For those who already train, better equipment and longer opening hours can make exercise easier to keep up. Local competitions and school programmes that use the same buildings can also pull in younger people. The mistake is to treat the building as a complete strategy. Health is shaped by what people do on ordinary Tuesdays: how they travel, what they eat, how long they sit, and whether their streets feel safe enough to walk. A gym membership cannot cancel out a diet built on cheap ultra-processed food, or a commute that involves two hours in a car.

**D**
A more realistic approach is to treat facilities as one tool among several. Cities that have seen modest improvements usually combine three things. First, they put everyday movement back into normal life: walking or cycling to work, stairs instead of lifts, parks that people actually cross on the way to the shops. Researchers often call this **incidental** activity, because it happens while people are doing something else. Second, they make food environments less hostile — for example by limiting the density of fast-food outlets near schools. Third, they still improve sports facilities, but they design programmes for beginners, not only for people who already look like athletes. Used this way, a new pool or sports hall can help. Used alone, it is unlikely to shift national health statistics.

**E**
The argument, then, is not “facilities versus everything else”. Facilities matter, but they cannot replace broader policy. If the political goal is a healthier population rather than a photogenic opening ceremony, governments need both the building and the boring work that surrounds it: transport, food, time, and the confidence to walk in.
